This blog post you're reading right now? It probably traveled through a tube thinner than a garden hose, sitting 4,000m deep on the ocean floor.

Not buried. Not anchored. Just laid on the seabed in the dark.

So what happens when it breaks?

First, engineers shoot a pulse of light down the fiber. When it hits the fault, some light bounces back. Measure the delay, you've got the location within 10 meters across a 6,000km cable.

That technique is called **OTDR** (Optical Time-Domain Reflectometry). Think sonar, but with light inside glass.

Then the repair depends on where the break is:

- **Shallow water (near shore)**, divers go down, retrieve the cable, and bring it up for repair.
- **Mid-depth**, ROVs (remotely operated underwater vehicles) locate and recover the cable.
- **Deep ocean (4,000m+)**, a repair ship drags a grapnel hook across the ocean floor, snags the cable, and hoists it all the way to the surface.

In every case, the actual splice happens on the ship's deck. Engineers cut out the damaged section and fuse in a new one with sub-micron precision.

There are only about **60 of these repair ships** in the entire world.

- Total cost per repair: $1-3 million.
- Time from fault to fix: weeks to months.
- These cables carry 95% of intercontinental internet traffic.

The internet feels instant and invisible. The infrastructure behind it is anything but.
